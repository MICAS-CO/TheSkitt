I’ll take the hard line: the underlying product looks much more visually credible when it behaves like a serious teal-on-black clinical training interface than when it tries to prove it is a pixel RPG. The data UI is already a strong identity. The pixel layer is not fatal, but right now it is uneven: charming in the asset library, useful in small icons, weakest when promoted to “main character art.”

The pixel-art aesthetic partly lands. The red pixel cross/logo works. The tiny staff sprites in the history-card list work because they are small, functional, and give speaker/source identity without stealing attention. The status icons work because pixel art maps well to glyphs: red flag, trap, countdown, citation. The asset library also shows there is real authorship here, not a downloaded retro skin.

But the Mrs Patel portrait in the encounter screen does not yet earn its space. It sits in a fake monitor frame like a Game Boy cutscene, while the rest of the page is a precise, modern clinical cockpit. It does not add enough emotional read, clinical state, or narrative texture to justify the visual weight. Disco Elysium earns portraiture because the faces are psychologically rich and stylistically inseparable from the writing. Here, the portrait is too simple to carry that burden. It reads as “we have pixel patients” more than “this patient is changing in front of you.” The surface would currently be cleaner with a smaller portrait, or with the portrait integrated into a clinical observation panel rather than staged as a monitor display.

The Phaser department hub should be reduced to static. Not cut conceptually — the department map is useful. It gives spatial memory: resus, majors, paeds, minors, triage, relatives’ room. That helps a trainee think like they are on a floor rather than inside isolated quizzes. But the live walk-around does not earn a 1.5MB dependency in its current form. It is the oldest-feeling screen by a mile: coloured rectangles, tiny avatar, crude labels, no atmosphere, no payoff. Movement is not interaction unless movement creates decisions, interruptions, proximity cues, or time pressure. If walking to triage takes three seconds but clicking the card is cognitively equivalent, the walk cycle is ceremony. Make it a static tactical board: same ED geography, current cases placed in bays, status chips, timers, maybe “go to patient” buttons. That would preserve the useful spatial metaphor without pretending this is a top-down RPG.

The encounter screen’s information density is appropriate for the genre, but the hierarchy is not ruthless enough. This is a cognitive-load training sim; it should feel busy. The clock, tabs, NEWS2, vitals, ECG, log, and history actions are all defensible. What is not defensible is duplication and ornamental scale. The right rail has portrait, NEWS2, vitals paper, monitor toggle, and then the shift log lower down. That side panel becomes a museum of widgets. The patient portrait is the obvious thing to shrink or subordinate. NEWS2 and vitals are clinically actionable; the portrait is not yet. I would make the right rail a compact “patient state” module: small portrait thumbnail, NEWS2, current vitals, trend, last event. Then put ECG or paper chart only when clinically relevant or when opened. The encounter can remain dense, but every object needs a job.

The semantic-colour contract mostly pays off, and that is one of the strongest parts of the build. The green NEWS2 0, muted neutral triaged chip, teal borders, and black ECG panel all read cleanly. The restraint gives confidence. This does not look like a random Bootstrap app. It looks like someone decided what each colour means.

But the contract leaks in amber and red. Amber appears as “TODAY’S SHIFT,” e-portfolio block accent, awaiting clinician flag, and bed-state amber. That weakens the claim that amber is only trap. If amber is operational status, then say that; if it is trap-only, stop using it as a warm UI accent. Red also starts to drift: the logo/cross is fine, resus stripe is fine, but red pills like “pick,” “decide,” and “core actions open” make red feel like generic urgency rather than clinical danger. The discipline is close enough to be worth protecting. Do not let convenience accents dissolve it.

Typography is good, maybe one font too many. Space Grotesk plus Manrope plus JetBrains Mono is a coherent brand: modern, technical, legible, slightly game-like. The mono is especially strong for timers, logs, vitals, ECG summaries, and clinical machine text. The all-caps tracked labels are overused but broadly effective. Caveat is the danger. Handwritten nursing notes could be brilliant if used like marginal human evidence — a scribbled drug chart note, a “daughter says mum never complains” annotation, a crossed-out handover line. If it is used as decorative whimsy, it will read twee fast. In a UK ED training tool, handwritten should mean “messy human system,” not “cute journal.”

Brand identity is almost there but undermined by small signals. “The Skitt” has personality and local texture. The red pixel cross is memorable. The teal clinical interface feels credible. The footer disclaimer is appropriate. But “v0.0.1” in the persistent footer screams prototype and should not be visible to learners. The TheCase.Report lockup is too small and slightly awkward next to the stronger The Skitt masthead. On a desktop it reads like a serious indie FRCEM project. On a phone? Not yet. The screenshots are wide, roomy, and heavily two-column. A UK ED trainee would want the content on their phone, but the visual system currently feels designed for a laptop during study time, not a five-minute break on shift.

The single highest-leverage visual improvement: redesign the encounter right rail into one disciplined clinical-state card. Smaller portrait, no fake monitor theatre, vitals/NEWS/trend/log hierarchy tightened, ECG/paper chart only when relevant or expanded. This would improve the most important screen, reduce visual noise, and clarify whether pixel art has a job. If the portrait remains, make it clinically responsive: sweat, pallor, distress, oxygen mask, posture, daughter present. Then the pixels earn their keep.

What I would cut: the live Phaser walk-around. Keep the ED map idea, lose the movement. Right now it is the clearest example of cost exceeding value, and it makes the product look less mature than the rest of the interface.
